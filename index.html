macOS · open source · nothing to install on the phone

Your phone is
the trackpad.

PadRemote turns a phone or tablet's touchscreen into a wireless trackpad for your Mac. The computer runs a small menu-bar app. The phone just opens a web page — no app store, no account, and nothing leaves your Wi-Fi.

git clone https://github.com/padremote/padremote cd padremote && ./install.sh

Builds the app, installs it to ~/Applications, and launches it. Needs Rust and Node.

How to install Read the docs

  1. 01

    Install the Mac app

    One command. It lives in the menu bar — no window, no Dock icon.

  2. 02

    Grant Accessibility

    macOS asks once. An app that moves your cursor needs it, and macOS owns that decision.

  3. 03

    Point a camera at the QR

    Tap the link that appears. The whole phone screen becomes the trackpad.

It behaves like the trackpad you already use.

Not because it guesses well — because it reads your Mac's own trackpad settings and copies them, and keeps re-reading them while it runs.

Mirrors your Mac

Your settings, not its defaults

On startup — and every half second after — PadRemote reads your real macOS trackpad settings and matches them. Natural scrolling, tap to click, secondary click, three-finger tap, the swipe gestures: if you changed it in System Settings, PadRemote follows within a second or two. No restart.

Each setting says where its value came from, so “why does it do that?” is answered on the page rather than in a config file.

Not copied, and it says so: Force Click and rotate (no phone hardware, no way to synthesize them), and tracking speed — Apple's acceleration curve is private, so speed is an approximation you can tune.

Press and hold

Selecting text without a button

A trackpad has a button to hold. A phone has neither a button nor a pressure sensor, so a long press stands in for both. Rest one finger; a ring charges around it, and when it fills the mouse button is held. Slide to select, lift to release.

The signal is drawn where your hand isn't: brackets close in on the corners of the screen while the press charges, and the whole rim blooms white the moment the drag arms. That reads out of the corner of your eye — which is where you will see it, because your eyes are on the other screen.

A click sound on every phone, and a buzz where the browser allows one. The animation is the part that always arrives.

Gestures

Shown, then named

Every gesture carries a small drawing of the pad with a finger on it for each one the gesture takes, and the selected gesture is demonstrated in the middle of the page. Words are exact and slow; a picture answers “which one is that?” at a glance.

Each swipe direction is its own setting — three fingers up and three fingers down are two, not one — and every action a gesture can take is listed rather than hidden in a menu. Four-finger tap is the one gesture your Mac doesn't have, so it arrives unbound and is yours to assign.

Settings

On whichever screen is nearer

The same settings page opens from the menu bar or from the pad in your hand, live on both at once, saving as you change something.

The phone's own sheet is an override, not a copy: each control shows your computer's value and says “matching your computer” underneath. Change one and it becomes yours — on that device only — until you hand it back. A tablet and a phone can each keep their own feel.

Underneath it is still a JSON file, hot-reloaded, if you would rather edit that.

Pairing

The QR is a key, not just an address

The code carries a 128-bit secret, and every connection — the trackpad, the settings page, the diagnostics view — answers an HMAC-SHA256 challenge before the desktop reads a single touch. Another device on your Wi-Fi cannot drive your cursor just by finding the port.

A connection from a web page is refused during that handshake, which matters more than it sounds: a WebSocket is not subject to the same-origin policy, so without the check any tab open in any browser on your machine could have moved your cursor.

Each phone then swaps the code for a key of its own and forgets the code — so Forget beside one device revokes that phone alone, and it stays revoked after a restart.

More than one device

A phone and a tablet, taking turns

Scan the same code with a second device and both stay connected. They share the one cursor and take turns: it goes to whoever touches next, as soon as the other one stops. Nothing to press, nothing to close.

The device that is waiting says so, and names the one that has it. Every device that has ever paired is listed — including the one that is switched off, which is usually the one you want to forget.

One process, one port

The page your phone loads is compiled into the app and served on the same port the phone then opens its socket on. There is no second server to start and nothing to leave running in a terminal.

Reconnects by itself

A dropped connection retries on a 1 s → 10 s backoff, and losing it always releases a held button, so a walk out of Wi-Fi range never leaves the mouse down.

One session per device

Open the pad in a second tab and the first stands down and says where its trackpad went, rather than the two racing each other for the cursor.

Change your mind mid-gesture

Start moving with one finger, lay a second down, and it becomes a scroll — re-measured from where your fingers are now, exactly as on the trackpad.

The phone decides nothing

It reports raw touch points as 14-byte records, one batch per animation frame. All interpretation happens on the desktop, so behaviour is tuned without redeploying anything.

Nothing phones home

No account, no telemetry, no cloud. Traffic goes from your phone to your computer over your own network, and a check in CI guards it.

Install

You need a Mac, a phone on the same Wi-Fi, and — because building it yourself is the only option today — Rust and Node. Nothing is installed on the phone.

01

Build and install

git clone https://github.com/padremote/padremote
cd padremote
./install.sh

Builds the phone page into the app, installs it to ~/Applications where Spotlight finds it, offers to start it at login, and launches it. It runs in the menu bar with no Dock icon.

--no-login
the same, without the login item
--login
add it without being asked
--uninstall
remove the app; settings and pairing are kept

02

Grant Accessibility

System Settings → Privacy & Security → Accessibility → enable PadRemote, then launch it again. Without it macOS accepts every injected event and quietly discards it, which looks exactly like a broken app — so PadRemote refuses to start instead.

The grant is remembered per app (one granted to your terminal does nothing for PadRemote.app) and per build. With no code-signing certificate the bundle is signed ad-hoc, so macOS treats a rebuild as a different app: the box stays ticked and the app is still refused. install.sh clears the stale grant and prints the one-time recipe that makes grants stick.

03

Pair your phone

Menu-bar icon → Connect a device… opens a page with a QR code. Open the Camera app on your phone, point it at the code — don't take a photo — and tap the link.

The moment the phone connects, the code gives way to Move a finger on the trackpad. Add another device brings it back when a second phone or tablet needs it.

Don't post the QR anywhere: until that first connection it is the key to your computer.

Or run it from source

The page is compiled into the app, so build it first. On first run macOS needs Accessibility for whatever runs the app — in development, your terminal.

cd web && npm install && npm run build
cargo run --manifest-path desktop/Cargo.toml

It prints the address to open on your phone. Useful flags: --dry-run (recognise gestures, inject nothing), --headless, --port, --unpair.

Where it is today

Everything on this page works now. These are the things that don't yet, said plainly rather than left for you to discover.

  • No signed installer

    Building it yourself is the only option: a copy built on another Mac would be blocked by Gatekeeper. A notarized .dmg is planned.

  • No TLS yet

    The link is plain ws://, so someone already positioned to read your Wi-Fi can see the touch stream — they cannot open a session of their own. What is defended and what is not is written down in the threat model.

  • macOS only, in practice

    The gesture engine is OS-agnostic and the Windows and Linux backends are written and unit-tested — but they have never been run on real hardware. Treat them as unfinished.

Questions

The ones that come up first, and the ones that come up at the worst moment.

Does my phone need an app? +

No. The phone opens a web page that your computer serves. Any modern phone or tablet browser works — iPhone, iPad, Android — and there is nothing to install, update or sign in to on that side.

Can someone else on my Wi-Fi move my cursor? +

No. Finding the port is not enough: every connection answers an HMAC-SHA256 challenge over the 128-bit secret carried in the QR before the desktop reads a single touch. Connections from web pages are refused during that handshake.

What is not defended yet is confidentiality — the link is plain ws://, so someone already able to read your Wi-Fi traffic can watch the touch stream go past. They still cannot open a session.

The cursor doesn't move at all. +

The phone will tell you which of the two it is — read the line under the status dot first, because it names the fix.

Almost always it is Accessibility permission: System Settings → Privacy & Security → Accessibility → enable PadRemote. The cursor starts moving the moment you tick the box, with nothing to restart on either end.

The other cause is a copy started with --dry-run, which reads every gesture and moves nothing on purpose.

Why does macOS ask for Accessibility again after an update? +

Because without a code-signing certificate the app is signed ad-hoc, and that identity is the hash of that exact binary. A rebuild is therefore a different app as far as macOS is concerned, and the old grant is dead while the checkbox still looks enabled.

install.sh clears the stale grant so macOS asks again, and prints the one-time Keychain recipe that makes grants survive rebuilds.

The phone says “this site can't be reached”. +

PadRemote serves the page itself, so if the app is not running there is nothing to load. Look for the ●● icon in the menu bar.

From the Mac, curl -I http://localhost:8787/ answering 200 means the page is being served and the problem is between the phone and the Mac — a different Wi-Fi, or a guest network with client isolation.

It worked yesterday and now the QR doesn't. +

Your Mac's Wi-Fi address changed, and the old code points at nothing. Open Connect a device… and re-scan whatever code is on screen — the page is drawn from wherever your computer is at that moment, and one left open reloads itself when the address moves.

Your phone stays paired across the move, because the pairing code does not change when the address does. To stop it recurring, give the Mac a reserved IP in your router.

Scrolling goes the wrong way. +

PadRemote copies your Mac's Scrolling direction: Natural, so it should already match. If it doesn't, this phone has taken the setting over: open Settings on the pad and look under Natural scrolling.

“Matching your computer” means the Mac decides it — change it in System Settings → Trackpad. “Match my computer” means this phone is overriding; tap to hand it back. The override is local to that phone either way.

The pad says someone else is using this computer. +

Two devices are connected, which is fine — they take turns. To take over, just touch the pad: the cursor comes to you as soon as the other one stops, about a third of a second after it lifts its fingers.

If nothing you do ever takes effect, check the name in the message. It may be a forgotten tab open on another device; closing it hands the cursor over for good.

My iPhone never buzzes when a drag starts. +

It can't: iOS Safari has no vibration API, and many tablets have no vibration motor at all. That is why a buzz is a bonus and never the signal — the animation and the click sound are what always arrive.

On a device that should vibrate but doesn't, open /haptics.html on it: it says whether the browser refused the call or handed it to the device, which are two very different problems.

Why is zoom steppy? +

No public macOS API can synthesize a real magnify gesture, so zoom is sent as ⌘+ / ⌘−. It steps rather than glides, and only works in apps that have a zoom command.

Rotate can't be delivered at all: PadRemote could detect the twist; nothing could inject it.

Does it work on Windows or Linux? +

Not yet, honestly. The gesture engine is pure and OS-agnostic, and the Windows and Linux backends are written, compiled and unit-tested in CI — but they have never been run on real hardware, so nobody has watched a cursor move on either.

Where do my settings live? +

~/Library/Application Support/PadRemote/config.json, hot-reloaded — edit it by hand and feel the change within about half a second, no restart. The settings page prints the path at the bottom.

Setting "followSystem": false stops PadRemote copying your Mac and pins behaviour to the file.
